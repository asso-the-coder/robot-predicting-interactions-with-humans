\section*{Introduction}

This project grew out of my \href{https://drive.google.com/file/d/1Sqb0hDDnlDuVB5g78kB9woW4czeqSbG5/view?usp=sharing}{research on humanoid robotics}. Although that work focuses on audio models and conversation in a controlled setting, I realized that interaction would feel much more natural if a robot could first recognize whether a nearby person intends to engage and then initiate the conversation itself. This is difficult in public spaces: people approach, pause, look around, or pass by in varied ways within a noisy, comparatively chaotic physical world. I therefore chose machine learning over classical rule-based methods because hand-set thresholds for distance, speed, or orientation are unlikely to generalize across unfamiliar people and environments. This project treats early engagement prediction as supervised binary classification: given a short robot-centric sequence observed before engagement begins, the model predicts whether that person will interact within the next four seconds. Machine learning can instead infer intent from temporal combinations of distance, speed, direction, body orientation, and pose. A reliable prediction could help a public-facing robot greet an interested person promptly while avoiding unwanted interruptions. The practical scope is deliberately low-risk and excludes surveillance or consequential decisions.

\section*{Background and Related Work}

PAR-D defines the closest prior task and contributes public-robot interaction trajectories, behavioral labels, and computational and human baselines \citep{thompson2024pard}. JRDB provides robot-centric multimodal perception in populated indoor and outdoor environments \citep{martinmartin2019jrdb}; JRDB-Social extends it with individual, group, and social-context annotations \citep{jahangard2024jrdbsocial}. These datasets show the value and difficulty of perception from a moving or public robot, but PAR-D is the only one used for training here. Social LSTM established recurrent modeling of pedestrian trajectories and interactions \citep{alahi2016social}. The original LSTM addresses long temporal dependencies through gated recurrent state \citep{hochreiter1997lstm}. Transformers replace recurrence with self-attention and require an explicit representation of temporal position \citep{vaswani2017attention}. This project compares both sequence families with a simpler logistic model, focusing on early interaction intent rather than trajectory generation.

\section*{Data Processing}

The CC0 PAR-D Shutter release contains 1,059 scenario CSV files from two university lobbies, sampled at 5 Hz \citep{thompson2024pard}. The downloaded release contains no camera photographs or video frames of people: each model input is a numerical time series of robot-relative distance, pedestrian velocity, movement direction, head and body orientation, and 3D skeleton-joint coordinates. Auditing found 139,092 rows, 337 columns, and 2,429 unique $(\mathrm{lobby},\mathrm{scenario},\mathrm{person})$ tracks over 13 recording dates. We adopt PAR-D's released four-second prediction horizon: the authors chose this cutoff based on prior evidence that engagement can be detected approximately three to four seconds before it begins \citep{thompson2024pard,bohus2009engagement}. Thus, a target is positive when the released time-to-engagement field is in $[0,4]$ seconds at the final observation. This horizon offers potential response time but is not evidence that four seconds gives higher user satisfaction than three. Any window containing current engagement is rejected. Label-construction fields, button events, robot actions, speech, and future-state columns are excluded.

Dates are assigned before windows are constructed: nine dates for training, August 11 and 18 for validation, and August 8 and 23 for final test. This prevents overlapping frames from the same collection day entering multiple splits. Two-second windows contain 10 contiguous frames; gaps above 0.31 seconds are rejected. Missing velocity at the first track sample is imputed, and every numeric feature is standardized using training data statistics only. The resulting 50,157 windows are strongly imbalanced (5.0\% positive).

\section*{Main Architecture and Baseline}

\textbf{Main architecture.} The main architecture is a two-layer LSTM with 64 hidden units per layer and dropout 0.2. The last-layer hidden state passes through dropout and a linear head to produce one raw logit. It has 58,689 trainable parameters for pose input.

A Transformer was added afterwards as an exploratory comparison; because it was built after the test split had been read, it is evaluated on validation data only. It projects each 33-dimensional frame to 64 dimensions, prepends a learned classification token, and adds sinusoidal positions. Its two pre-normalized encoder layers each use four attention heads, a 128-dimensional GELU feed-forward block \citep{hendrycks2016gelu}, and dropout 0.25; a normalized classification-token embedding produces the logit. It has 69,505 parameters.

Both networks use AdamW, batches of 512, gradient clipping at 1.0, and weighted binary cross entropy with a training-only positive weight of 20.83. The LSTM uses learning rate $10^{-3}$, weight decay $10^{-4}$, at most 25 epochs, and early-stopping patience 5; the Transformer uses $5\times10^{-4}$, $10^{-3}$, at most 30 epochs, and patience 7. Checkpoints, early stopping, and probability thresholds are selected by validation F1. Sigmoid is applied only for metrics.

\textbf{Baseline.} The baseline is class-balanced logistic regression ($C=1$). For every temporal input feature it receives the final value, mean, standard deviation, and net change across the window. The selected orientation model therefore has 48 inputs plus an intercept. A majority-class predictor is also reported as a sanity check.

\section*{Quantitative Results}

At seed 360, the pose LSTM gave the best validation F1, 0.345, compared with 0.284 for orientation logistic regression. Motion, orientation, and pose LSTM F1 scores were 0.289, 0.289, and 0.345. Pose-window F1 was 0.274, 0.345, and 0.284 for one, two, and three seconds, so two seconds was selected. Longer context also reduced the number of eligible windows.

\section*{Generalization to Unseen Recording Days}

The 5,587 test windows come from two recording dates that were excluded from preprocessing statistics, feature and window selection, checkpoints, thresholds, and hyperparameters. They are new temporal samples from the dataset's two existing locations, not independently collected cross-site data. This tests date shift within known locations, so no cross-site generalization is claimed. Test confusion matrices are $[[4675,433],[343,136]]$ for logistic regression and $[[4771,337],[435,44]]$ for the LSTM, ordered as $[[TN,FP],[FN,TP]]$.

\textbf{Post-hoc analysis of the frozen predictions.} The analyses below describe the already frozen test predictions and were not used to select or alter any model or threshold. Threshold-free average precision is 0.197 for logistic regression and 0.119 for the LSTM against a chance level of 0.086 (Figure~\ref{fig:posthoc}, left), so the ranking does not depend on the chosen thresholds. Joining each positive window with the released time-to-engagement shows that recall falls as the prediction becomes earlier: logistic regression recalls 51\% of windows ending within one second of engagement but only 14--17\% of those ending two to four seconds before it (Figure~\ref{fig:posthoc}, right).

\section*{Discussion}

The main result is a failure to generalize, not a neural-model improvement. The best-seed LSTM appeared to gain 0.060 validation F1 over logistic regression, but two additional seeds reduced the LSTM mean to 0.273. On unseen dates its recall fell to 9.2\%, while the simpler baseline retained 28.4\% recall. The rising validation loss in Figure~\ref{fig:curves}, high variance across seeds, and lobby/date subgroup differences point to overfitting and temporal distribution shift.

This task is difficult for three reasons. First, positives form only 5\% of windows. Second, the target is future engagement behavior, an imperfect proxy for internal intent. Similar approach-and-stop motion can receive opposite labels. Third, overlapping windows create many samples but fewer independent behaviors. Rich pose improved one LSTM seed but hurt logistic regression, so additional coordinates are not automatically informative. The lead-time analysis also shows that most of the usable signal arrives in the final second before engagement, so a four-second horizon is ambitious for these features. Future work should add independent locations and more positive tracks, evaluate at the track level, calibrate probabilities across dates, and test whether longer histories help once they do not reduce the dataset so sharply.

\section*{Conclusion}

Among the three learned models, orientation logistic regression is best supported: it achieved 86.1\% test accuracy and 0.260 positive-class F1, versus 86.2\% accuracy and 0.102 F1 for the pose LSTM. Because accuracy is inflated by class imbalance (only 5\% of windows are positive), logistic regression's higher F1 makes it preferable. The validation-only Transformer was not tested after the frozen evaluation, so no Transformer test accuracy or test ranking is claimed.

\section*{Ethical Considerations}

Public-space observation, even if audioless, creates privacy and consent concerns even when released data are de-identified. Kinect pose noise, behavioral imbalance, and two campus lobbies may also produce uneven errors for people or behaviors absent from training. A false positive can cause unwanted attention; a false negative can make the robot unresponsive. The system should therefore control only reversible actions such as a neutral greeting or waiting, retain no identifying imagery, expose an uncertainty-based option to abstain, and never support surveillance, access, employment, or safety-critical decisions.
